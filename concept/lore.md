# The shepherds

The cosmology, as it now stands. This is the fiction. Where it touches the rules,
the rules are named — and in one important place the rules already do what the
fiction says, and have done all along without anyone writing down why.

---

## Where the powers came from

Nowhere. They were not sent, they have no errand, and there is no one above them.

They **manifested out of the land and the living things in it** — a slope, a
watershed, a stand of old trees, the animals in it, the weather that comes over
the ridge. Something in a place accumulated until there was someone there. They
are of the valley in the way a river is of the valley, and they are about as
answerable to anything.

The nearest comparison is a Maia, with the parentage removed. No Valar, no
commission, no West. Just a place that became a person.

## The wild folk

The powers put people into the blessed country. This is a founder myth and it is
meant to be told like one.

They are **few, and they are wild**. They live in the forest and the hills, they
take the year as it comes, they build nothing that lasts, and — the load-bearing
part — **they can hear.** Not because they are holy. Because they are few and the
country is quiet, and there is nothing between them and the god but air.

They resemble their power more than they resemble what their descendants will
become. That is why the powers like them. A god walking among wild folk is walking
among a smaller version of itself.

**This is the state the powers would preserve if it were up to them.** Left alone,
a power would keep a valley of a few hundred people who hear it, forever, and
would count that a life well spent.

## Why they do not

Because they know what they are looking at.

They know the humans. They know that being few and quiet is not where this goes —
that people will multiply, band together, put their creativity into the material
world, build things that hold, and make new things happen that a god could not
have made and cannot imagine. Human organisation is the next thing, and it will
lead to things after that.

So the powers do it anyway. They sow the seed, they make the ground habitable in
the spirit as well as the body, and they raise a people they know will outgrow
them. Like a parent, who is not being destroyed by the child's competence.

They will step aside. They hope to be remembered.

> **You can only be remembered by the thing that deafens you.**

The sentence does not change. What changes is who is speaking it. It was being
read as a complaint. It is a decision.

## What this changes

### The tone stops being self-pity

The old framing had the fade happening *to* you: you point at a hillside, and
later you cannot, and this is sad. That version has a whine in it.

In this version you did it. You blessed the ground knowing what grows there. The
melancholy survives — more of it, if anything — but it is the dignified kind, and
it is the kind that can carry forty turns.

`concept/concept.md` says **not a tragedy on rails**. This is what makes that
true, rather than a thing the document asserts.

### The central dilemma gets much sharper

It has been stated as *blessing versus farmland*, which is a resource tension.
Underneath it there is now a better one:

> **The people you love are not the people who will remember you.**

Wild folk hear you and build nothing. Their descendants will build something that
lasts and will not hear a word of it. Every settlement you found is a choice to
convert the first into the second, on purpose, because only the second can carry a
memory at scale.

That is the same trade the rules already implement. It is a much harder thing to
do to someone once they have faces.

It also gives **the Seventy-Seven** its meaning. `design/rules.md` §4 asks that the
custom read as something the people keep themselves rather than a rule imposed
from outside. Now it does: they split before they cross the line because some part
of them remembers what they were, and does not want to stop being able to hear.

### Competition still works, and is better

If the fade is accepted, why fight?

Because *whose* people inherit the valley, and *whose* name survives in what they
build, is entirely undecided. You are not fighting the fade. You are two parents
in one valley, and only so much of it can be raised your way. The rival is no
longer a generic opponent — it is someone doing exactly what you are doing, for
exactly your reasons, and the ground is finite.

This makes the **Absorbed** and **Syncretism** endings in OP-15 land far harder.
Surviving as a feast day in someone else's calendar is a different kind of wound
when the calendar belongs to a colleague.

## The disagreement

**The powers do not agree with each other about any of this**, and that is the
part worth building on. What is written above is not the cosmology of the world.
It is the majority theology, and the majority is not obviously right.

The question they argue about is simple and unresolved:

> **Was settling the right thing to allow?**

The convinced say yes: people were always going to build, the world is larger for
it, and a power that stands in the way is a power indulging itself at its
children's expense.

The refusers say no, and their case is not sentimental. They can feel what
sedentary life carries in with it — the grain store and the seasons of hunger
after it, the counting, the levy, the walls, the man who owns the field and the
man who works it. Wild folk are hungry sometimes. Settled folk are hungry in
famines, and taxed in the good years, and made to march. A power that sees this
and blesses anyway is not being generous, it is being negligent. Better a few
hundred who hear you than ten thousand who are counted.

Neither has been proved right. Neither ever is.

### The roster is the argument

`concept/Player-character-inspiration/ancient_gods_roster.md` already groups
eighteen powers by archetype, and the archetypes turn out to be positions rather
than flavour. A stance column is wanted:

| Archetype | Count | Stance |
|---|---|---|
| **Wilderness** — Cernunnos, Potnia Theron, Tapio, Mielikki | 4 | **Refusers.** Keep them few and keep them listening. |
| **Early Civilization & Rivers** — Ningirsu, Nodens | 2 | **Convinced.** Irrigation, survey, the city as the point. |
| **Fertility & Earth** — Asherah, Dumuzi, Mokosh, Pachamama, Epona, Snake Goddess | 6 | **The hinge, and the largest group.** Agriculture is both the blessing and the trap, and they are the only ones who cannot answer the question without contradicting themselves. |
| **Storm & Sky** — Taranis, Baal Hadad, Ukko, Chaac | 4 | **The third answer.** Not yes and not no: *let them keep animals and keep moving.* Herders' gods. The objection was never to settling; it was to being asked to hold still. |
| **Underworld & Hidden Powers** — Veles, Velnias | 2 | Think it is a mistake, and would rather it proceeded anyway so they can be there for the end of it. |

**The third answer was a blank until August 2026.** Storm & Sky were written as
indifferent because nobody had a position for them, and indifference is not a
theology. Pastoralism is the position. The refusers want the people kept few, the
convinced want them counted, and these want them **moving** — the only one of the
three that says the land should not be held at all, and the only one that leaves
graves rather than archives. See the pastoralists in `registers/ideas.md`, and
OP-12.

**This gives the doctrines in `design/rules.md` §9 a reason to exist.** They are
currently mechanical labels — *cities*, *bands*, *mixed*, *passive*. Under this
they are convictions, and the strategy follows from the belief rather than the
other way round. Choosing an opponent becomes choosing what you are arguing with.

### And it puts the balance table on the spot

If a doctrine is a theology, then the win rates in §10 are the game's own verdict
on the argument. Right now the game has one, and it is emphatic: **Cities wins 78%
of everything it plays; Bands wins 24%.**

That is the fiat outcome this document warns against, and it is not hypothetical —
it is the current build. As it stands the game says the convinced were right and
the refusers were fools, and it says so in a dropdown labelled with their names.

**This is what OP-06 is actually about**, and it is a larger problem than "is an
ascetic strategy viable". A refuser should be able to keep four hundred wild folk
who hear it, build almost nothing, and win. If the balance says otherwise, that is
a balance problem and not a moral.

Write the powers as certain. Do not let the rules be.
